The Moats: what protects the Bonnie & Clyde link

Read time: 12 minutes. One win: you can explain every layer of defence, outside in, and say exactly what each one does — and does not — prove.

Rule of the house: a claim without a test is marketing. Every moat below has a ticket, and the ones marked “planned” are not claims yet. Say what a proof does not cover; that is what separates documentation from advertising.

0. The map is not a stack

Picture a castle. Most moats are rings an attacker must cross on the way in. But one thing — getting the source code or a configured board's firmware image — is not an inner ring at all. It is the road to the castle: a different axis.

That distinction matters. If someone has your source, they get the defaults, not your key (you changed it in one line). If someone has your built UF2, they have your key, because it is compiled in as literal bytes. Forward secrecy does not close that road; it just makes a key stolen on that road useless for old traffic.

Evidence: firmware/proto/proto_netcfg.h, tools/gen_psk.ps1, tools/provision_pair.ps1. Residual risk: ticket 38. Fix for old traffic: ticket 31.

1. The air path, outside → inside

Moat 1 — Camouflage SSID (done, cosmetic)

  • What it is: the network is named TMOBILE-1219, not SECRET-MESSAGE-NETWORK.
  • Significance: buys a few seconds of not being interesting to a casual scanner. Protects nothing cryptographically.

Moat 2 — WPA2, the first real moat (done)

  • What it is: Wi-Fi password 12345678, WPA2-AES. Stops strangers associating and reading frames.
  • Significance: the outer cryptographic layer. Weak password, deliberately — message security does not live here.
  • Boundary: pktmon capture proved that even after this layer is decrypted, our payload is still ciphertext. That is the defence-in-depth claim, not a WPA2 strength claim.

Moat 3 — BSSID identity binding (done, hardware verified — ticket 39)

  • What it is: the STA is locked to AP BSSID 88:a2:9e:5b:ff:69, checked at runtime with cyw43_wifi_get_bssid.
  • Significance: cloning the SSID + password is not enough. The impostor has the wrong hardware address and the STA leaves.

Moat 4 — App PSK + KDF (done, hardware verified — tickets 10–14)

  • What it is: a 32-byte key, pc_kdf with salt BONNIE-CLYDE-v1, deriving separate directional keys.
  • Significance: this is the moat that made the pktmon capture unreadable, and the moat the ESP32 proved against a no-PSK observer.
  • Boundary: static — one leaked PSK breaks all history. That is exactly why moat 7 exists.

Moat 5 — Key confirmation handshake (done — tickets 11, 31)

  • What it is: HELLO / HELLO_ACK carry a confirmation tag.
  • Significance: wrong key ⇒ AUTH FAILED: HELLO bad tag, no LINK UP. An attacker without the key cannot complete the handshake.

Moat 6 — Per-frame encryption + replay window (done — tickets 12, 13)

  • What it is: Speck CTR + CMAC on every frame; a replay window rejects stale and duplicate sequence numbers.
  • Significance: tampering is detected (CRYPTO FAIL: bad tag) and recorded frames cannot be replayed to trick the receiver. T15/T16 passed on hardware.

Moat 7 — Forward secrecy (done, hardware verified — tickets 31, 37)

  • What it is: a fresh X25519 key pair per session; traffic keys are never stored.
  • Significance: a leaked static PSK can no longer decrypt recorded sessions. This is the moat that fixes the biggest hole in moat 4.
  • Boundary: it does not stop live impersonation by someone holding the PSK — they can participate in the exchange as a legitimate peer. The confirm tag MACs the first 8 bytes of the peer key, not all 32; the DATA tag authenticates the rest implicitly.

Moat 8 — Per-message ratchet (done, hardware verified — ticket 33)

  • What it is: traffic keys evolve per message within a session.
  • Significance: compromising one message key does not unroll the whole session. Limits blast radius forward in time.

Moat 9 — Masked header (done, hardware verified — ticket 34)

  • What it is: session id, sequence number and ACK bits are CTR-masked with ECDH-derived keys after the handshake. The separately-tagged proto_hdr envelope is host code, not on the wire.
  • Significance: a PSK-only leak cannot strip the mask. Type, length and timing stay visible, and the keystream repeats within one session — say masked, not “encrypted”.

Moat 10 — AEAD suite (done, hardware verified as an opt-in — ticket 32)

  • What it is: opt-in ChaCha20-Poly1305 via -DPHANTOM_USE_AEAD=1.
  • Significance: a modern, standard authenticated-encryption option alongside Speck for the PoC.

Moat 11 — Device zeroization (done, hardware verified on BYE — ticket 36)

  • What it is: wipe derived keys on BYE or a 2.5 s two-button chord.
  • Significance: innermost crypto moat — protects secrets already inside the castle after an orderly teardown.

Moat 12 — LPI / decoy chatter (app layer done; detection report pending — ticket 29)

  • What it is: constant-rate-ish PING decoys with bounded jitter; SFO as application-layer telemetry.
  • Significance: makes traffic less patterned. Explicitly not a covert claim — the CYW43 MAC/PHY is untouched.

Moat 13 — Hardware hardening (research only — ticket 35)

  • What it is: RP2350 OTP secure boot and debug-port disable, proven on a spare board only.
  • Significance: raises the cost of firmware replacement. Does not stop flash readout of a key — do not oversell it.

Moat 14 — Independent review (not started — ticket 37)

  • What it is: an external person reviews the design.
  • Significance: not a technical moat — it is the inspection that tells you the other moats are real.

2. The sentence to put in docs

Outer moats (already dug): camouflage → WPA2 → BSSID lock → app PSK/KDF → key confirmation → replay window.
Inner moats (dug and hardware-verified): forward secrecy → per-message ratchet → masked header → AEAD opt-in → zeroization → LPI decoys.
Inspection (planned): hardware hardening on a spare board + independent review (PR open).
Honest boundary: a leaked PSK still permits live impersonation; forward secrecy only removes the recorded-history damage. The confirm tag MACs 8 of 32 public-key bytes, and the header mask keystream repeats within a session — neither is claimed as full binding or header confidentiality.

3. The one-line override — why open source is fine

A person who clones the repo and changes the PSK, SSID/password and enrolled BSSID in firmware/proto/netcfg_local.h / proto_netcfg.h before flashing two fresh Picos has a private link we cannot read. The published values are defaults, not secrets. That is the intended distribution model — and it works today, independent of forward secrecy.

The repo being public therefore does not hand an attacker your key. It hands them the demo key. The one-line override is what moves the secret out of the repository and into the hands of the person flashing the boards.

Correct framing: open source + one-line override ⇒ private against remote attackers. Physical/binary access to your specific configured board still exposes the key — that is the residual risk in ticket 38, and the reason moat 7 is worth building.

4. Test yourself

Does the ESP32 capture prove WPA2 is strong?
No. It proves a passive observer with no credentials sees ciphertext at RF. It says nothing about WPA2 strength — in fact the pktmon capture assumes WPA2 was already decrypted and the app payload was still unreadable.
Does forward secrecy make the shared PSK unnecessary?
No. It is still the bootstrap. It only means a leaked PSK cannot decrypt old traffic. Live impersonation with the PSK remains possible until per-device identities are added (not in 31–39).
Why is moat 0 on a different axis?
Because it is the road to the castle, not a ring inside it. An attacker with your built UF2 skips every RF moat and goes straight to the compiled key. Forward secrecy limits the damage to new sessions, not old ones.
What does moat 13 actually protect against?
Firmware replacement and debug access — not key extraction from flash. Never claim it stops physical readout.
